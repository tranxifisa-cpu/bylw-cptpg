\section{Introduction}\label{sec:introduction}

Portfolio advice is inherently sequential and path dependent.  An investor who reaches the same current wealth after a rally and drawdown may evaluate the next risky allocation very differently from an investor who reaches that wealth after a loss and recovery.  In human-facing financial systems, this distinction matters because a policy that is attractive under expected return can be rejected once recent gains, losses, and reference levels are taken into account.  Cumulative prospect theory (\CPT) provides a canonical description of such behavior through reference dependence, asymmetric valuation of gains and losses, and nonlinear probability weighting \citep{kahneman1979prospect,tversky1992advances}.  These features make \CPT particularly relevant to sequential portfolio choice, but they also turn the learning objective into a nonlinear functional of the entire trajectory-outcome distribution rather than an additive one-period reward.

A growing literature has brought this distributional objective into reinforcement learning.  Early work formulated prediction and control under \CPT and established stochastic optimization procedures for prospect-theoretic objectives \citep{prashanth2016cumulative,jie2018stochastic}.  Most closely related to this paper, \citet{lepel2026policygradients}---the current v5 version of the core \CPT policy-gradient reference---derive a first-order policy-gradient theorem for finite-horizon \CPT-RL, construct an order-statistics Monte Carlo estimator, and prove convergence and sample-complexity guarantees for first-order stationarity.  This establishes a principled foundation for \CPT policy optimization.  Its reference point, however, enters as a fixed element of the \CPT criterion rather than an endogenous state that is updated by the realized wealth path.

Behavioral evidence and recent portfolio models point in the opposite direction.  Reference points adapt after realized gains and losses, with systematic asymmetry in the speed of adjustment \citep{arkes2008reference,arkes2010cross}; adaptive and path-dependent references can materially change realization and portfolio decisions \citep{he2019realization,qin2022realization,gao2024multiperiod}.  The simplest example already changes the optimization problem.  Starting from normalized wealth $1$, consider a rise to $1.4$ followed by a fall to $1.2$, versus a fall to $0.8$ followed by a recovery to $1.2$.  The terminal wealth is identical, but an upward-adjusted reference can place the first path in the loss domain while a lower reference places the second in the gain domain.  Portfolio-level evidence that gain--loss framing affects the disposition effect reinforces the importance of this state dependence \citep{an2024portfolio}, while recent Chinese retail-investor evidence shows that disposition behavior also varies with information and investor characteristics \citep{chen2025xueqiu}.

Once the reference point is made endogenous, two difficulties that are separate under a fixed target become coupled.  First, the \CPT gradient depends on the derivative of a probability-weighting function evaluated at an unknown outcome distribution.  Finite-rank plug-in evaluation therefore introduces a nonlinear estimation problem before the policy can be updated.  Second, the target itself moves: market conditions generate exogenous variation, while the policy changes wealth, wealth changes the reference point, and the reference point changes the next \CPT objective.  Convergence to a single stationary point is no longer the appropriate criterion.  The relevant question is whether a feasible first-order policy can track a sequence of moving stationary conditions, and how its tracking error decomposes into target variation and statistical estimation error.

This paper develops \emph{Dynamic-Reference Cumulative Prospect Policy Gradient} (\DRCPTPG) for this setting.  The portfolio policy is a factor-parametrized Dirichlet distribution, which produces long-only simplex-valued allocations while keeping the parameter dimension tied to interpretable characteristics rather than the number of stocks.  The reference state follows an asymmetric update after realized wealth changes.  For learning, a centered leave-one-out score statistic is combined with an independent, untruncated randomized full/half correction.  The resulting gradient output is exactly unbiased for the frozen regularized \CPT target, has finite variance and finite expected trajectory cost, admits a studentized central limit theorem, and attains the fixed-dimensional minimax trajectory-budget rate.  The online layer then uses a normalized projected update and evaluates tracking through the projected residual and dynamic local regret (DLR).

The analysis is organized around three linked questions.  The first is statistical: can the nonlinear \CPT policy gradient be estimated without finite-sample bias while retaining usable variance, inference, and budget guarantees?  The second is dynamic: under what variation and simulation-error conditions does online updating keep the projected first-order residual small as the market and reference state evolve?  The third is behavioral and empirical: does dynamic reference formation produce the path dependence and trading asymmetries predicted by behavioral finance, and do the resulting distinctions remain visible when the model is taken from controlled simulations to real A-share portfolios, held-out investor behavior, and heterogeneous retail-agent interactions?  This separation lets each layer of evidence test a distinct part of the argument rather than using portfolio returns as a proxy for estimator correctness or using behavioral fit as a proxy for optimization quality.

The theoretical results answer the first two questions.  We derive the regularized \CPT score-gradient identity; prove the finite-sample bias order of the centered leave-one-out base; construct an exactly unbiased estimator with a finite $M^{-1}$ variance bound, asymptotic normality, and a minimax-optimal $\Theta(\mathsf B^{-1})$ fixed-dimensional trajectory-budget rate; and establish a bound on the average expected squared projected residual for the online algorithm.  For a fixed window, dynamic local regret is controlled up to a window constant by the cumulative squared projected residual, and a local error-bound condition converts the same residual control into distance to the moving stationary set.  When objective variation is sublinear and the sampling and simulation errors vanish in Ces\`aro average, the expected average DLR vanishes.

The empirical program mirrors this logic.  Path experiments first isolate the role of reference history in portfolio ranking.  Fixed-target experiments then evaluate the estimator independently of online state propagation, continuous slow-variation experiments test online tracking, and a structured reference-mechanism ablation followed by horizon and optimizer sweeps separates mechanism effects from implementation robustness.  The external-validity layers then move from controlled objects to market and behavioral outcomes.  In the time-ordered A-share replay, DRCPT-PG has the highest mean Sharpe ratio ($0.856$) and the lowest mean maximum drawdown ($11.67\%$), turnover ($10.87$), and transaction cost ($2.42\%$) across the five policy-gradient methods.  On $71{,}854$ held-out investor decisions, the asymmetric reference representation gives the best log loss, Brier score, AUROC, and AUPRC among the four matched predictive specifications; the paired bootstrap log-loss improvement over no reference is $0.0249$ with a 95\% interval $[0.0214,0.0287]$.  Finally, replaying the E5 recommendations through four investor types calibrated from real behavior places DRCPT-PG first on adoption-weighted interaction value across all five advisers in every type.  This progression connects theorem-level objects---gradient error, projected residual, and DLR---to economic, behavioral, and interaction outcomes while keeping their estimands distinct.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=2em]
\item \textbf{Endogenous-reference \CPT policy optimization.}  We formulate a finite-horizon portfolio objective in which the psychological reference point is part of the evolving state rather than a fixed hyperparameter.  The model yields explicit path dependence and a decomposition of objective variation into non-reference and reference components; fixed-reference \CPT-PG is recovered as a special case.
\item \textbf{Debiased gradient estimation with statistical guarantees.}  We establish the regularized \CPT score-gradient identity and construct a centered leave-one-out estimator with an independent randomized raw correction.  The estimator is exactly unbiased, has finite expected cost and finite variance, obeys a multivariate central limit theorem, and attains the fixed-dimensional minimax rate under the same expected complete-trajectory budget.
\item \textbf{Online first-order tracking for a moving nonconvex target.}  We analyze a normalized projected update through the projected residual.  The resulting bound separates objective variation, replication error, and frozen-simulator error; it implies a square-first DLR bound and, when a local error-bound geometry holds, tracking of the moving stationary set.
\item \textbf{Layered external validity from market replay to behavior and interaction.}  Beyond the theory-aligned E1--E4 sequence, a fixed-universe 300-stock A-share replay places DRCPT-PG at the strongest joint risk-adjusted and trading-efficiency point across the five policy-gradient methods; a matched held-out behavior test shows stable incremental predictive value from asymmetric reference representation; and a calibrated four-type interaction replay ranks DRCPT-PG first on adoption-weighted interaction value across all five advisers in every investor type.  These layers extend the same reference mechanism without using portfolio return, predictive fit, or simulated satisfaction as substitutes for the estimator and tracking diagnostics.
\end{enumerate}
